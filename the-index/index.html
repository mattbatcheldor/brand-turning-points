About this archive

Brand Turning Points started with a question about a Sony advert. I remembered thousands of coloured balls bouncing down a San Francisco street, and I wanted to understand why the idea had stayed with me for so long.

Six editions later, it is an archive of 90 cases from 1958 to 2025. Every case has a credited image, a watch link, a source for its headline evidence and my own take. The series moves from how brands earn attention, in Fame first, to what gives people a reason to believe them, in Product first.

It is not a ranking. I rate each case on the strength of its public evidence, and that rating says more about what was measured and published than about what worked. Read the conclusions as hypotheses to take into a brief and test.

03 / The lessons

Six lessons that hold across editions

None of these belongs to one edition. Each is backed by cases from at least two angles. Choose a case to read it in full.

Show, don’t tell.

Say the uncomfortable thing first.

Reframed weakness turns up in 12 cases across four editions, and the two recoveries in Edition 05 use the same move. Naming the problem makes the answer believable.

Give people something back.

The data and machine ideas people loved gave something back: a year in music, a star for a corner shop, a billboard of their own. Work that took something away met resistance.

05

Don’t change what people feel they own.

Five brands in Edition 05 changed a formula, a carton, a logo or a name, and all five changed it back. MINI is the exception that proves it: it changed almost everything and kept the bit people loved.

06 / What’s missing

What the series doesn’t cover yet

Where the work ran

32 cases, 36%, started in the UK or Ireland, and 23, 26%, in North America. Asia has 6, Latin America 1 and Africa 1. The IPA databank is the richest source of proof, and it is British.

When

52 of the 90 cases, 58%, date from 2010 or later. Only 3 come from before 1970, because older campaigns rarely left a public record.

What the proof looks like

22 of 90 cases have a modelled effect. 34 show a sales trend, 23 are archival only, and survey evidence is the rarest, with 3.

Who it is about

Most cases are big brands with big budgets. Small businesses, charities and public services appear, but rarely, and they may have the most to teach.
